The evidence

The need is in
the research.

Peer-reviewed studies, government health agencies and the surgeons’ own professional bodies all point the same way: theatres can be loud enough to damage hearing, and noise gets in the way of clear communication.

What the authorities say

Regulators and professional bodies agree.

The law on workplace noise already applies to hospitals. Government health agencies and surgeons’ own organisations are now calling for action in theatre.

Health and Safety Executive (UK)

Action at 80 dB, protection compulsory at 85 dB, a hard limit at 87 dB.

Under the Control of Noise at Work Regulations 2005, employers must assess the risk once average daily exposure reaches 80 dB(A), make hearing protection compulsory at 85 dB(A), and never let exposure exceed 87 dB(A). There are also limits for sudden peaks of noise.

HSE: employers’ legal duties →

NIOSH, US Centers for Disease Control

Put orthopaedic surgeons on a hearing-loss prevention programme.

After measuring noise during knee replacement surgery at a US hospital, the US government’s occupational health agency recommended annual hearing tests, training, quieter tools and hearing protection for surgeons.

NIOSH Health Hazard Evaluation →

American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons

Noise in the orthopaedic theatre can cause cumulative hearing loss.

In October 2026 the AAOS warned that orthopaedic theatres often exceed workplace noise limits, that repeated exposure can add up to permanent hearing loss and tinnitus, and that the risk is still underrecognised.

AAOS Now, October 2026 →

World Health Organization

Hearing loss is largely preventable.

The WHO’s first World Report on Hearing projects that nearly 2.5 billion people will live with some hearing loss by 2050, and calls for prevention, including protection from loud noise at work.

WHO news release →

The research

Staff, communication and patients.

Theatre staff

The warning has been in the journals since 1991.

A UK study in the Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery measured powered orthopaedic tools above recommended levels, then tested 27 senior orthopaedic staff: half showed signs of noise-induced hearing loss. The authors called for ear defenders and quieter instruments.

J Bone Joint Surg Br, 1991 →

Communication

Noise makes it harder for surgeons to understand speech.

A study in the Journal of the American College of Surgeons tested surgeons’ word recognition and found that background noise in theatre measurably reduces how well they understand what is said to them.

J Am Coll Surg, 2013 →

Awake patients

Music lowers anxiety and pain around surgery.

A meta-analysis of 92 randomised controlled trials in the British Journal of Surgery found that music before, during or after surgery reduces patients’ anxiety and pain.

Br J Surg, 2018 →

Patients and power tools

Protect patients’ hearing too, especially older patients.

Measuring noise from powered tools in orthopaedic theatres, researchers concluded that patients, particularly the elderly, should have hearing protection during these operations.

Int J Clin Pract, 2008 →
What works. A 2026 systematic review, reported by the AAOS, found that hearing protection with filters that let speech through reduced noise exposure while keeping theatre communication intact. Surgical hood systems helped little, and noise-cancelling headphones gave mixed results.
The balanced view. Not every study finds harm. A small UK study of 18 orthopaedic staff found only minimal, temporary hearing changes after a working day (J Laryngol Otol, 2004). The risk comes mainly from short, very loud bursts of noise that add up over a career, which is why measuring exposure matters as much as protection.
